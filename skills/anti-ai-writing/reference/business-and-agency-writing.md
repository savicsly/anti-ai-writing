# Business & Agency Writing: Proposals, Client Messages, Case Studies

Habits that make client-facing business writing read as templated, with fixes. Use this when editing proposals, scopes of work, lead replies, discovery questions, status updates, invoices and payment reminders, case studies, pitch copy and agency or consultancy websites.

The same rule applies as everywhere in this skill: specificity beats polish. In sales and delivery writing the specifics are the client's name, their problem in their words, a number, a date, and a decision.

---

## What to protect (never "humanise" these)

- **Contract and legal clauses.** Wording was chosen on purpose; rewording can change obligations. Edit only the plain-language summary around them.
- **Figures and terms:** prices, taxes, dates, durations, payment milestones, quantities, SLAs.
- **Names, titles, company details, requirement and ticket IDs, file names, URLs, email addresses.**
- **Quotes from clients** and testimonials. Do not smooth, shorten or "improve" them.
- **Code, commands, config and API names.**
- **Mandated formats** such as invoices, tender responses and compliance forms.

If a sentence mixes protected content and loose prose, rewrite only the prose around it.

## Proposals and scopes of work

| Template habit | Why it reads as generic | Fix |
|----------------|------------------------|-----|
| "We are excited to present our comprehensive solution tailored to your unique needs" | Fits every client | Open with their situation: "Your dispatchers re-key orders from WhatsApp into the ERP twice a day." |
| "Our innovative, scalable, end-to-end platform" | Adjective stack, no claim | Say what it does and for whom, with a measurable outcome |
| Three-benefit bullets under every section | Triad by default | Use the real number of points, drop the weak ones |
| "Our team of experts will leverage best-in-class technologies" | Empty proof | Name the stack, the roles, the person who leads |
| "Seamless integration with your existing systems" | Overpromise | Name the systems, the integration method, the known risk |
| "Why choose us?" with five virtues | Competitor swap-test fails | One or two verifiable facts: a delivered project, a metric, a constraint you handle well |
| Closing "We look forward to partnering with you on this exciting journey" | Stock closer | A concrete next step and date: "If you approve the discovery sprint by Friday 14 November, we start on the 17th." |

Executive summary test: a busy decision-maker should learn the problem, the plan, the cost and the timeline in half a page, without a single adjective doing the work of a number.

## Lead replies and client emails

- Open with the answer or the reason you are writing, not with thanks for reaching out at length.
- Reflect their situation in one specific sentence so they know a person read the message.
- Ask 3 to 5 questions that change scope or price, not a long questionnaire.
- Offer two or three concrete call times.
- Skip stacks of politeness ("I hope this finds you well", "please do not hesitate", "looking forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience") and the stock ending.
- Keep length proportional to theirs. A two-line enquiry gets a short reply.

**Regional register.** Courteous formality is normal and respectful in much of West African business correspondence. Phrases such as "Kindly find attached", "Please find attached", "Dear Sir/Madam" or "Best regards" are not AI tells in that context, and non-native or formal English is not evidence of machine writing (see Liang et al., 2023, in detection-and-models.md). Match the recipient's formality; remove only padding that carries no meaning. Do not flatten a respectful tone into something that reads as curt.

## Payment reminders and chasers

- State the invoice number, amount, due date and how to pay in the first lines.
- One clear ask with a date: "Can you confirm payment by Friday?"
- Escalate tone through firmness and consequences cited from the contract, not through adjectives or exclamation marks.
- Avoid the "just a friendly reminder" template when the invoice is a month overdue.

---

## Status updates and delivery notes

- Lead with the state: on track, at risk or blocked, and why.
- Done, next, blockers, decisions needed from the client, each with names and dates.
- Report what happened, not effort: "Checkout works on Android 10 and up; iOS Safari fails on saved cards, fix due Thursday."
- No fractal summaries: do not preview, report and then recap the same content.

---

## Case studies

- Start with the client's problem and one hard number (before and after), not with "We were thrilled to partner with".
- Say what was actually built and one decision that was hard, including a trade-off or a thing that went wrong. Light, honest friction is what makes these credible.
- Attribute testimonials as the source gave them. Never invent or polish a quote, a metric or a client.
- Avoid the "challenge, solution, results" scaffold only if it hides the story; it is fine as headings when the content under each is specific.

---

## Agency and consultancy websites

- Swap test: if the line works unchanged on a competitor's site, rewrite it.
- Replace service-list adjectives (*innovative*, *end-to-end*, *bespoke*, *cutting-edge*) with the deliverable, the user and the outcome.
- Prefer concrete proof (a named case, a figure, a constraint such as low-bandwidth users or data-residency rules) to trust badges and vague "trusted by leading companies".
- One primary call to action per page.

---

## Editing checklist (business writing)

- [ ] Opens with the client's situation, a decision or a fact, not enthusiasm
- [ ] Every claim has a name, number, date or example behind it
- [ ] Prices, dates, IDs and legal wording untouched
- [ ] Triads and stacked adjectives cut to what the content needs
- [ ] One clear next step with a date
- [ ] Tone matches the recipient and the culture of the correspondence
- [ ] Reads like one specific person wrote it to one specific reader
