# AI Sentence Patterns, Paragraph Structure & Rhythm

How LLMs build sentences and paragraphs — and how to break the metronome. Use this file when prose feels polished but lifeless, overly symmetrical, or "perfectly structured."

---

## Why cadence matters

Bloomberry's research frames AI recognizability as stacked signals: elevated vocabulary, low-information transitions, symmetrical rhetorical forms, **uniform cadence**, predictable openings/closings, and low-specificity conclusions. Editors often notice sentence lengths clustering in a mid-range band (roughly 12–18 words) with little "burstiness" — the human habit of mixing short punches with longer runs.

## Classic sentence patterns

### 1. Three-part rhythm (tricolon)

AI loves balanced triples:

- "Clear. Concise. Compelling."
- "Plan the work, do the work, review the work."

**Human fix:** keep one strong point; drop artificial symmetry. Or make the third item uneven on purpose.

### 2. Contrast / antithesis frame

The hallmark template:

- "It's not just about X. It's about Y."
- "This isn't X — it's Y."
- "Not X. Y."

**AI example:** "It's not just about increasing sales. It's about providing customer value first."

**Human rewrite:** "Don't chase sales volume alone — make sure customers get real value."

**Why it feels fake:** preachy antithesis is a high-frequency AI rhetorical move.

### 3. List expansion

Announce completeness, then enumerate:

- "There are three key factors: A, B, and C."
- Each factor gets a nearly identical paragraph shape.

**Human fix:** lead with the most important factor; merge weak ones; skip the "there are N" preamble unless the count itself matters.

### 4. Definition pattern

- "X is a [adjective] [noun] that [does Y]."
- "At its core, X is…"

Useful once. Deadly when every section opens this way.

### 5. Problem → Solution

Rigid two-beat:

1. Broad problem statement ("Organizations struggle to…")
2. Product/approach as savior ("That's where X comes in…")

**Human fix:** start mid-problem with a concrete incident; let the solution earn its place.

### 6. Hook → Explanation

- Hook: "Here's the thing…" / "Let's be honest…" / "Most people…"
- Then a tidy explanation paragraph.

**Human fix:** put the claim first without the fake-intimacy wrapper.

### 7. Hedge-then-assertion

- "While X has limitations, Y remains essential."
- "To some extent… However…"

AI hedges to stay safe, then asserts. Humans with evidence often just assert (and note limits once).

### 8. Motivational cadence

- "Most people wait for permission. You don't need it."
- Short claim → punchy imperative.

Fine for a pep talk. Weird in a technical memo.

### 9. Aphorism / maxim packing

- "Clarity is speed. Less is more."

Sounds like a tagline deck. Prefer one concrete observation.

### 10. Repeated clause structures

Parallel openings across paragraphs:

- "In this section, we…"
- "In this section, we…"
- "In this section, we…"

Or synonym cycling to avoid repetition while keeping the same shape.

**Human fix:** vary openings; allow one paragraph to start mid-thought.

| Qualification cascade | Hedge stack before claim | "While results vary and context matters, generally speaking…" |

## Paragraph patterns

### Equal-length blocks

AI often produces paragraphs of similar size, each:

1. Topic sentence
2. Explanation
3. Mini-summary or moral

Humans digress, cut short, or let one paragraph run long.

### Information ordering

Typical AI order:

1. Broad context
2. Definition
3. Benefits / challenges (balanced)
4. Summary

Human order is messier: anecdote → claim → caveat → detail.

### Repetition habits

- Restate the prompt keywords
- Rephrase the same point with synonyms
- End each section by restating the section thesis

### Summary habits

- Mid-article mini-conclusions
- "In summary" / "Overall" paragraph closers
- Explicit lesson statements ("The key insight is…")

**Rule:** if the paragraph already made the point, don't summarize it again.

### Typical AI article skeleton

```text
Title / Hook
  → Intro (generic context)
  → Body point 1 (explanation + tidy close)
  → Body point 2 (same shape)
  → Body point 3 (same shape)
  → Conclusion (summary + takeaway)
```

Break it: uneven section lengths, one vivid example instead of three equal points, an ending that doesn't restate.

## Writing rhythm

### Sentence-length distribution

| Tendency | AI-leaning | Human-leaning |
|----------|------------|---------------|
| Length | Mid-range uniformity | Mix of short / medium / long |
| Fragments | Rare | Occasional, intentional |
| Run-ons | Rare | Occasional in informal voice |
| Interruptions | Rare | Asides, dashes used sparingly, parentheses |

### Alternating long/short (false "voice")

Some models simulate burstiness with a mechanical long–short–long pattern. Real variation is irregular, driven by meaning — not a metronome.

### Uniform cadence tells

- Every sentence similar weight
- No incomplete thoughts
- No mid-sentence course corrections
- No "wait — actually…" moments

### Lexical diversity caveat

LLMs can show high type–token ratios (synonym hopping) on mundane topics. Humans often reuse the ordinary word. Don't "diversify" vocabulary for its own sake.

### Perfection as a tell

Zero typos, zero voice slips, perfect parallelism everywhere can itself feel synthetic. Natural writing has texture: a fragment, a contraction, a slightly uneven list.

## Before / after (structure & rhythm)

**AI (even rhythm):**

> The project will require coordination. Each team must deliver on time. Without this, deadlines will slip.

**Human:**

> We'll need coordination — every team delivering on time is crucial. Otherwise we miss deadlines.

**AI (contrast frame + tidy close):**

> It's not just about shipping features. It's about delivering value. In summary, focus on outcomes.

**Human:**

> Shipping features isn't enough if nobody uses them. Measure outcomes.

## Editing checklist (structure & rhythm)

- [ ] Break at least one symmetrical triple or antithesis frame
- [ ] Vary paragraph lengths; allow one short punch paragraph
- [ ] Cut mid-section summary sentences that restate the obvious
- [ ] Mix sentence lengths; add one intentional fragment if the genre allows
- [ ] Replace "There are N key…" preambles with the first real point
- [ ] Read aloud — if it sounds like a slide deck, rewrite
